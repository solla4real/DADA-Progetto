# HRTEM / FFT zone-axis indexing

Method for turning a table of measured FFT spots (d-spacing, azimuthal angle, amplitude) into a verified zone-axis hkl indexing against a candidate crystal structure (from a CIF or equivalent atomic model). Distilled from a long, iterative real indexing session — every rule below fixes a mistake that actually happened.

## 1. Prepare the data
- Convert all d-spacings to one unit (Å) immediately.
- Use **absolute angle to a fixed reference axis** (e.g. "degrees to x-axis") for every spot, not chained relative angles ("degrees to spot 1"). Relative angles compound rounding/measurement error across the chain.

## 2. Friedel-pair first, always
Real spots come in antipodal pairs: same amplitude, angle ~180° apart, related by (h,k,l) ↔ (−h,−k,−l). Identify pairs by matching amplitude (should be nearly identical) before doing anything else, then average the pair's d to cancel random noise. Never d-match raw individual spots independently — noise on a single spot can push it outside a tight tolerance even when the underlying family is obviously correct.

**A computed FFT power spectrum of a real-valued image is mathematically exactly centrosymmetric** (I(g)=I(−g), always — a property of the Fourier transform of real data, not a physics assumption). Every final hkl assignment must be reported as an explicit (h,k,l)/(−h,−k,−l) pair, never collapsed to a single "family" label without stating the exact negated partner — if asked to verify centrosymmetry, print both members of every pair and confirm the negation directly.

## 3. Candidate search: d AND angle, never d alone
In any lattice with symmetry higher than triclinic, several distinct hkl share **identical d** (e.g. orthorhombic (h,0,l),(−h,0,l),(h,0,−l),(−h,0,−l) are exactly degenerate in d). d-matching alone cannot disambiguate them and will silently assign the same hkl to spots at completely different measured angles — a hard sign of a bug if it happens (check for this: no two spots with different measured angles should ever land on the same hkl).

Fix: pick one strong, unambiguous family as the angular calibration reference (assign it its ideal angle, e.g. 0°), derive the calibration offset from it, then require every other family's calculated ideal angle (from the candidate hkl's true geometry) to match the calibration-corrected measured angle within a few degrees — in addition to the d tolerance (~3%).

## 4. Verify with exact vector closure in hkl-space
Once 2 independent basis families are identified, every other family should equal an **exact integer combination** of the basis families' hkl (not of the raw noisy measured vectors). E.g. C = A+B, D = B−A, G = −2A. This is much stronger evidence than d/angle fit alone and should always be checked and reported — it is the strongest single indicator that a solution is genuinely self-consistent rather than a coincidental per-spot fit.

## 4b. No harmonic without its allowed fundamental — check this for EVERY assigned hkl, not just the basis vectors
If a spot is assigned (nh, nk, nl) — i.e. its indices share a common factor n>1 — check every intermediate divisor step down to the irreducible direction (h,k,l)/gcd. For each intermediate multiple, compute |F|² from the candidate structure. **If any intermediate multiple is symmetry-allowed (|F|²≠0) but is NOT itself one of the observed/assigned spots, the assignment is almost certainly wrong.** You cannot see the 2nd (or higher) harmonic of a direction while the allowed fundamental (or an allowed intermediate) is simply absent from the pattern — a real crystal doesn't skip an allowed lower-order reflection and then show the doubled one (e.g. seeing (400) clearly without (200), when (200) is symmetry-allowed, is a red flag, not a valid result). This check must run on *every* assigned reflection, not only the two chosen basis vectors — it is easy to verify vector closure (section 4) and structure-factor non-zero (section 5) for a spot and still miss that its reduced fundamental is unaccounted for.

When this check fails and no clean alternative hkl exists within normal d/angle tolerance: **stop forcing a fit.** Say so explicitly and concisely — e.g. "Non riesco a indicizzare questo spot in modo pulito: dovrei vedere anche il riflesso X (permesso, ma assente dal pattern), quindi c'è qualcosa che non va" — and give your best-guess hypotheses for the cause (wrong candidate structure/phase, wrong basis-vector choice for the zone, or a genuinely unexplained spot) rather than reporting the best-scoring-but-physically-inconsistent numerical fit as if it were a confirmed answer. A numerically good d/angle match is not sufficient on its own if it fails this test. Do this proactively, without waiting for the user to catch the inconsistency.

## 5. Compute structure factors from real atomic positions
Compute |F(hkl)|² directly from the full symmetry-expanded atom list of the candidate structure (expand all atoms through every symmetry operator, then sum Z·occ·exp(2πi(hx+ky+lz)) — or use real atomic/electron scattering factors if available, not just atomic number Z, since Z-based amplitudes only give qualitatively-right allowed/forbidden but can badly mis-rank *relative* intensities). Never trust a tabulated general extinction rule alone — special positions can behave differently. A candidate hkl with a great d/angle fit but |F|²≈0 is not valid; move to the next candidate. Reuse this same |F|² computation for the harmonic/fundamental check in section 4b — don't skip it there just because the d/angle fit looked good.

## 6. Kinematic vs. dynamical intensity — don't force a fit, don't hand-wave either
Measured HRTEM/FFT amplitude order will often NOT match calculated |F|² order — electron diffraction is subject to dynamical (multiple-scattering) effects that kinematic theory doesn't predict. This is expected and normal (cite general dynamical electron diffraction theory / double-diffraction, e.g. Dwyer et al., Acta Cryst. A69 (2013), or Cowley's "Diffraction Physics"), NOT evidence the indexing is wrong. But don't just gesture at "dynamical effects" — check whether the anomalously-strong-but-kinematically-weak reflection's hkl equals the exact vector sum of two other strong reflections already in the pattern (a double-diffraction pathway, e.g. E = A+B). If it does, say so explicitly — that's a specific, falsifiable explanation, not a hand-wave. Note: dynamical/double-diffraction effects explain unexpected *intensity* at an allowed position — they do NOT excuse a missing allowed fundamental under an observed harmonic (section 4b); that failure mode points to a wrong hkl assignment, not to diffraction physics.

## 7. Zone axis via the Weiss zone law — use bare integers, not scaled vectors
Given two reflections (h1,k1,l1), (h2,k2,l2) known to be in the same zone, the zone axis [UVW] is the cross product of the **bare integer hkl triples**:
```
U = k1*l2 - l1*k2
V = l1*h2 - h1*l2
W = h1*k2 - k1*h2
```
This relation involves no cell parameters at all and holds for any cell shape, including triclinic. **Do not** take the cross product of the Cartesian reciprocal vectors (h/a, k/b, l/c) and then divide back out by 1/a,1/b,1/c component-wise — this introduces cell-shape-dependent scaling factors (e.g. a/(bc) instead of 1) that corrupt the result for any non-cubic cell. It can appear to "work" by pure luck when the true zone axis happens to have two components equal to zero (0 × wrong-scale-factor is still 0), which can mask the bug — always derive/check the zone axis with the bare-integer formula.

## 8. Axis-convention mismatches between tools — the single costliest failure mode
When cross-checking against a CIF, a second script, or vendor simulation software, confirm which physical axis each tool calls "a" vs "b" vs "c" before comparing any hkl. A mismatch (e.g. one convention has a=6.861,b=8.987; another swaps them) makes the same physical reflection look like a completely different hkl in the two tools, and will look like a real contradiction until the convention is pinned down.

- Swapping which axis is "a" vs "b" means swapping **h and k together** — never permute the cell lengths without permuting the corresponding index labels (and the atomic fractional coordinates) the same way. Swapping only one is the most common and costly version of this bug.
- A zone-axis label from a tool (e.g. "[0 1 0]") is only meaningful once you know that tool's own axis order. Before trusting it, check that the format of its displayed reflections ("h,0,l" vs "0,k,l") is dimensionally consistent with which axis it declares as first/second — don't assume a simulated schematic's stated zone matches your own analysis without this check. When genuinely unsure, derive the zone axis independently from real, angle-resolved spot data (Weiss zone law, section 7) rather than trusting a tool's label at face value.
- The empirical, unambiguous test that a swapped-axis file/cell is physically equivalent to the original: fully expand both structures into P1 (all symmetry operators applied) and confirm the atom sets match after mapping coordinates back through the same permutation — do this check before ever trusting derived symmetry operators for a swapped/non-standard setting.

## 9. Deliver results as ONE auditable summary table — hkl, d-spacing error AND angular error together, every time
Always present final results as a single compact table, one row per spot (both members of every Friedel pair listed separately, not collapsed), with AT LEAST these columns: spot/family label, hkl, d_measured, d_calculated (calibration-corrected), **d-spacing %error**, ang_measured, ang_calculated, **angular error in degrees**. Both error columns are mandatory together in the same table — a table with only d-errors (angle errors left to prose, or omitted) is incomplete and must not be presented as the final deliverable; likewise never split them into two separate tables when one combined table is possible. Also include |F|² and, when available, measured amplitude as extra columns — the correlation (or lack of it, per section 6) between |F|² and amplitude is itself useful signal for the reader. State the global calibration factor (k_fit or equivalent) once, above or below the table, not repeated per row. This is the standard, non-optional final deliverable format for every indexing task — produce it even for a quick or partial result, and even when section 4b or section 10 flags some spots as unresolved (list those separately, clearly marked, rather than omitting them silently). Before presenting, run every row through section 4b (harmonic-without-fundamental check); a row that fails it does not belong in a "final" table presented as solved — flag it separately as unresolved instead of hiding it inside an otherwise-clean table.

## 10. ALWAYS simulate the derived zone axis and cross-check every assignment against it — mandatory, every time, not just when asked
Once a zone axis [UVW] is derived (section 7) and a final hkl assignment reached, do not stop there. Independently generate the full simulated reflection set for that exact zone axis from the candidate structure: enumerate every (h,k,l) with U·h+V·k+W·l=0 within a reasonable index range, compute d-spacing and |F|² for each (reusing section 5's structure-factor routine), and keep only the symmetry-allowed ones. This is the same computation a zone-axis simulation tool (e.g. a .uce-style software) performs, and it must be run as a required step of every indexing task, not offered as an optional extra or only produced when the user explicitly asks for a simulation.

Then verify every one of the final assigned hkl (all Friedel pairs, both signs) appears in this independently-generated simulated set at the correct relative position (same iso-(h or k or l) row/grouping structure, same relative spacing pattern). Report this cross-check explicitly in the final answer — e.g. as a small table or checklist mapping each assigned family to "present in simulated [UVW] pattern: yes/no" — alongside the auditable table from section 9. If the user also has independent vendor-software simulation output (a screenshot or export) for the same zone axis, compare against that directly instead of only the self-generated set, and call out explicitly whether every assigned reflection lands on a labeled position in it (as was done to confirm a real solution against a NaFePO4.uce-style grid) — this vendor cross-check, when available, is stronger evidence than the self-generated one and should be preferred, but the self-generated simulation must still always run as the baseline even when no vendor output is available.

An assignment that fails this cross-check (a family with no matching position in the simulated pattern) is not resolved — apply the same section 4b stop-and-flag discipline: say plainly which family doesn't reconcile and why, rather than presenting the indexing as complete.
